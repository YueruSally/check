# 8. Discussion

Four findings organise the interpretation of the results. First, gateway-node capacity reductions and regional inland-capacity reductions without additional sailing delay were absorbed within the tested West Coast configurations, whereas sufficiently long sailing delays introduced material NY–NJ use. Second, gateway use depended on the decision objective: the minimum-cost and minimum-time solutions did not always use the same gateways, and the exact internal solutions showed that the share changed with the imposed delivery-time requirement. Third, material diversion did not imply recovery of most disruption loss; at the first tested material-use conditions, only 0.38–3.04% of the cost loss and 0–1.13% of the time loss was recovered. Fourth, destination, demand intensity, and the temporal distribution of shipment releases changed both the amount and the first tested delay of diversion. These findings apply to three independently solved destination cases rather than to a joint network in which the destinations compete for a shared capacity pool.

## 8.1 Capacity location and sailing delay produce different responses

The results do not support treating all capacity restrictions and timetable disruptions as interchangeable. Reducing the common West Coast gateway-node capacity to 35% did not produce material NY–NJ use. Reducing together the four West Coast inland road and rail capacities serving each destination to 30% also produced no NY–NJ use when no additional sailing delay was imposed. When the 30% regional inland capacity was combined with a 168 h delay, Columbus assigned 5.724% to NY–NJ, still below the pre-specified 10% criterion. By contrast, long sailing delays produced material use for all three destinations in the concentrated-arrival reference case.

The binding-resource records clarify the scope of this contrast. In the Columbus regional-capacity experiment, several West Coast inland connections reached their daily limits in different periods while a small East Coast allocation appeared. In the local-capacity experiment, changing one diagnosed connection altered the NY–NJ share at the same sailing delay. These are observations about the location and timing of binding resources; they do not establish a universal rule that delay dominates capacity. The model uses fixed departures, earliest feasible service selection, and no endogenous queueing, so it represents reallocation among available services rather than congestion formation and recovery.

## 8.2 Gateway value depends on the performance objective

The exact endpoints and ε-constrained solutions show that an alternative gateway can have different values under different performance requirements. At Chicago 288 h, NY–NJ expanded the attainable delivery-time region: the restricted model could not satisfy the five strictest tested time limits even though it remained feasible when no additional time limit was imposed. At Columbus 240 h, both access designs were feasible at every tested internal limit, but allowing NY–NJ reduced cost at the same delivery-time requirement. The NY–NJ share rose from 3.63% to 29.03% across the eight internal Columbus limits.

This evidence is stronger than assigning one activation time to an entire destination. A cost-focused decision and a time-focused decision can use different gateway configurations under the same disruption. The relevant managerial question is therefore not only whether NY–NJ appears, but also which objective is being protected and what additional delivery-time requirement is imposed.

## 8.3 Diversion share and recovered disruption loss are distinct

The counterfactual comparison separates the amount diverted from the operational value of having the alternative gateway available. At the first tested material-use conditions, the cost-loss recovery fractions were 3.04% for Chicago, 2.44% for Memphis, and 0.38% for Columbus. The corresponding time-loss recovery fractions were 1.13%, 0.91%, and zero. At 336 h, the tested recovery fractions were higher for all three destinations, but they still varied by objective and destination. A large gateway share should therefore not be interpreted as recovery of a similarly large fraction of disruption loss.

The cost decomposition also shows that the source of value was not uniform. In Memphis, higher transport expenditure was offset by lower waiting, holding, and tardiness costs. Chicago and Columbus obtained different combinations of transport and schedule-related savings. The penalty sensitivity provides an additional qualification: the NY–NJ share changed non-monotonically as the penalty multiplier changed, while tardiness loss evaluated at the original shipment-specific rates declined monotonically. The share pattern therefore reflects changes in the ranking of discrete path and timetable configurations, not a monotonic gateway response to a single cost coefficient.

The matched zero-penalty references further show that the baseline gateway pattern depends materially on the cost specification. Without tardiness penalties, the undisturbed minimum-cost solutions already used NY–NJ, and the resulting recovery fractions were 91.00%, 11.05%, and 99.38% for Chicago, Memphis, and Columbus at the focal delays. These are sensitivity measures within a different objective specification, not evidence that disruption activated NY–NJ from the original zero-share baseline. The empirical basis for the tardiness penalty is therefore important to the external interpretation of the cost results.

## 8.4 Temporal demand structure limits the portability of a single threshold

The release-design experiment shows that the first tested material-use level is sensitive to how demand is distributed over time. The Chicago minimum-cost result remained at 288 h across the tested designs, but its minimum-time result moved from 288 h in the concentrated-arrival reference case to 336 h in all multi-wave instances. Memphis mostly exhibited the same movement, while Columbus varied more strongly. The repeated 32.66% allocation in the concentrated-arrival case was not preserved across the dispersed-release instances.

Because the release designs changed release timing, temporal concentration, competition for daily resources, and accessible service dates together, the experiment identifies sensitivity to the temporal distribution of demand rather than a separate causal effect of timetable phase. Likewise, the tested delay brackets are discrete scenario results and should not be presented as universal real-world switching times.

## 8.5 Managerial implications

Three implications follow. First, contingency plans should state the inland market, the protected objective, and the shipment-release profile instead of relying on a single port-disruption trigger. Second, access to NY–NJ can be valuable even when the recovered fraction of total disruption loss is modest, particularly when a strict delivery-time requirement would otherwise be unattainable. Third, operators should evaluate an allowed-gateway plan against a same-disruption restricted counterfactual and report cost and time separately. The model does not include contractual change costs, extra coordination costs, or market-price responses, so small modelled benefits should not automatically be interpreted as commercially sufficient.

## 8.6 Reliability and limitations

The final evidence uses all simple structural paths satisfying the stated leg, mode-change, and no-repeat limits. The principal results are based on exact lexicographic mixed-integer endpoints and exact ε-constrained solutions rather than stochastic search. Exactness is nevertheless conditional on the model definition: each scheduled arc uses the earliest departure that can be reached while satisfying its cutoff, and the model does not permit an active choice to wait for a later departure on the same structural path.

The case-study network has three West Coast ports represented as two gateway groups and one East Coast alternative, NY–NJ. Savannah, Norfolk, and other alternatives are outside the choice set. The demand, timetable, tariffs, and capacity availability form a constructed case study, and the three inland destinations are solved independently. Accordingly, the capacity parameters should be interpreted as capacity available to the modelled freight in each destination case, not as a jointly allocated representation of total facility capacity. Future work may examine shared-capacity multi-destination models, additional East Coast gateways, stochastic disruption duration, and implementation costs; those extensions constitute new research scopes rather than missing replications of the present experiment.
